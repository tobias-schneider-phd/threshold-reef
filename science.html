Background · Science · Design notes

Threshold Reef

A reef you can push past its tipping point

Tobias Schneider, PhD · Scientific Learning Experience Designer

Coral reefs are not a distant or abstract climate problem. They are living ecosystems already being pushed through repeated marine heatwaves and mass bleaching, with shrinking windows to recover in between. Each global bleaching event has reached further than the last: bleaching-level heat stress affected about 21 percent of the world's reef area in 1998, 37 percent in 2010, 68 percent across 2014 to 2017, and about 84 percent in the fourth and largest event, from 2023 to 2025, which struck reefs in more than 80 countries across all three ocean basins (ICRI 2025; NOAA Coral Reef Watch / NESDIS 2026). Individual heatwaves still come and go with seasons and currents, but they arrive closer together and last longer, and scientists now describe reefs as entering an era of near-annual bleaching. How much reef structure and biodiversity survive this century depends largely on how far the ocean warms: the IPCC projects that warm-water reefs would decline by a further 70 to 90 percent at 1.5°C of warming, and by more than 99 percent at 2°C (IPCC 2018).

🌊

Why this piece exists

Threshold Reef exists to make that fragility felt, not just stated. It is a piece of science communication, science for the living room, that lets a curious person experience one of the most important and least intuitive ideas in reef resilience: a reef can have more than one stable state, and the way back is not the way in. You move three pressures, watch a living reef respond in accelerated time, push it too far, and discover that easing off does not simply undo the damage.

It is first a learning experience and only second a model. My credo as a learning experience designer is to build worlds people can step into, make decisions, and see the consequences from the inside. The model underneath is the means, not the message: a transparent, scientifically grounded core that lets the experience rest on established reef science rather than on something I invented. This page is open about what that core does, what it simplifies, and where its limits are, because being honest about the simplifications is itself part of scientific integrity, and it is the part most science communication quietly skips.

What it is

A standalone, browser-based simulation of a coral reef under three pressures, built as science communication ("science for the living room"). Not a game with a score; an experience with a lesson.

Who it is for

In the simulation itself: a curious layperson, from interested adults to school and early-university students. Around it, on pages like this one: scientists and institutes who want to check that the science underneath is solid.

Format and tooling

A single standalone HTML file. Vanilla HTML, CSS and JavaScript, no build step, no frameworks, no external libraries. The reef is drawn procedurally as SVG and recoloured live, so bleaching is the coral itself losing its colour, not a swapped image.

Status

Built and playable. The model engine is complete, and its bistability and hysteresis are verified numerically (see the appendix).

🧭

How it teaches

The piece is a real dynamical simulation, not a reactive slideshow, and that distinction is the point. A reactive piece reads the sliders and paints a matching picture, so every change is instantly reversible and nothing can ever truly be lost. Threshold Reef instead carries genuine state that integrates over time, and that state has a tipping point. Once the reef falls into the algae-dominated basin, easing a stressor back partway does not bring it back; recovery needs conditions pushed well beyond the point where it tipped, and even then the reef returns slowly. That asymmetry is the lesson, and it emerges from the dynamics rather than from a scripted response.

The emotional high point is not the collapse. It is the moment after, when the player eases the pressure back to where the reef was thriving a minute ago and the reef stays down. That is when "tipping point" stops being a phrase and becomes something the person has felt.

Experience before explanation. The simulation teaches by consequence, not instruction; there is no "correct" or "incorrect", only a reef that thrives or collapses and a quiet narration that names what is happening and why.
Built around agency. The person is meant to feel able to act, not merely informed of a disaster; local protection visibly matters, the reef can recover within the simulation under sufficiently improved conditions, and Reset is always available.
Honest about its limits. Where the model simplifies, this page says so, in plain terms, below.

The reef is alive on screen: coral colonies of several growth forms sway, fish school and thin out as grazers are lost, the water turns murky as nutrients rise, and coral pales and bleaches as the water warms. A status badge moves through Healthy, Stressed, Bleaching and Critical, and short narration lines name what is happening, so the system tells its own story in plain language as it unfolds. An optional panel explains the science whenever the person reaches for it, ideally after they have felt the tipping point rather than before.

The model underneath, and why this one

I wanted the experience to rest on established science, not on a mechanism I made up, so the engine is based on the analytical core of the coral, macroalgae and turf model of Mumby, Hastings & Edwards (2007), a foundational result on the resilience of Caribbean reefs. That paper contains two models, a spatially explicit simulation and a reduced analytical model of three competing space-occupiers; this piece uses the reduced analytical model. Three groups compete for space on the reef floor, and their cover fractions sum to one:

Ccoral cover
Mmacroalgal cover
Tshort, grazed algal turf: the space coral and macroalgae compete over, taken as the remainder. In the original this is turf kept short by grazers, not bare rock.

The mechanism the model captures, and the reason it suits this story, is feedback and competition. Coral and macroalgae compete for the same space; herbivorous fish graze the turf and hold macroalgae in check; and when grazing falls, macroalgae can take over and lock the reef into an algae-dominated state. That structure is what lets the simulation show a genuine tipping point and hysteresis: two stable states, with recovery demanding more than simply reversing the decline.

Two honest notes for a reader who knows this model. First, temperature and nutrients are not state or control variables in the original analytical model. The three levers here are deliberate didactic extensions: fishing changes the grazing rate, nutrient pollution raises macroalgal growth and spread, and temperature raises coral mortality and adds a heat-stress term above a bleaching threshold. Second, Mumby et al. treat bleaching as an episode of acute coral mortality and disturbance, not as a separate temperature term in the equations; the explicit heat term here is again a teaching device. These are extensions added to carry the message, made openly rather than hidden.

A word of scientific honesty

The theory and mechanism of bistability and hysteresis on reefs are well grounded, but the empirical evidence for true hysteresis on real reefs is mixed, scale-dependent and not universal (Bruno et al. 2009; Dudgeon et al. 2010; Mumby, Steneck & Hastings 2013; Schmitt et al. 2019). This simulation therefore presents hysteresis as a well-founded mechanism and teaching frame, not as a settled law that holds identically on every reef.

The exact equations, parameters and the numerical check of the two tipping points are in the appendix, for anyone who wants to verify them.

⚖️

What is simplified, and why on purpose

This is a qualitative teaching model, not a research or forecasting tool. The simplifications are deliberate, and each one buys clarity:

One reef, not many reef types. The original model was parameterised for structurally complex, mid-depth Caribbean forereefs; this piece does not represent other habitats.
Abstract, accelerated time, not calendar time. There is no fixed time unit, no countdown and no endpoint; the "decade" in the intro is only a framing device.
Fishing is represented as loss of herbivory. Real fishing is broader, but the decisive effect in this modelled mechanism is the removal of grazers, the lever that controls the coral-to-algae tipping point here.
Pollution is represented as nutrient enrichment, as one clean lever. In reality nutrient effects are strongly context-dependent and usually entangled with herbivory (Rasher et al. 2012).
Bleaching is represented as heat-driven coral stress and mortality, not as a full physiological model. Real bleaching depends on cumulative heat, light, taxon, symbiont type, prior damage and follow-on processes such as disease.
Turf stands for cropped algal turf, not every non-coral state. Real reefs can also shift into states dominated by sponges, soft corals, corallimorpharians or urchin barrens (Norström et al. 2009).
Not represented at all: spatial structure, larval dispersal, sedimentation, ocean acidification, disease, species differences, and hydrodynamics.

The reason for holding the line is the same as the reason for the piece. The goal is not ecological completeness; it is to make one well-established idea, alternative stable states and the asymmetry of recovery, visible and legible to a non-specialist. Every extra variable would make the model more realistic and less teachable.

🔎

Limits of interpretation, and how this was built

Threshold Reef should be read as a conceptual instrument, not a prediction. It is built on the Caribbean-derived analytical core of a single model and is not calibrated to any particular reef, so the thresholds it shows are heuristic learning objects, not universal numbers. Region and habitat matter, and Caribbean values should not be sold as global defaults (Mumby et al. 2016). The cleanest one-line statement: it is modelled on a classic Caribbean coral and macroalgal mechanism that can produce bistability and hysteresis under certain conditions, and it serves understanding of feedbacks and tipping points, not prediction of specific reef trajectories.

A note on how this was built

My work here is as a Scientific Learning Experience Designer with a biology PhD, not as a reef modeller. The background in tropical marine ecology comes from graduate study in the ISATEC programme at the University of Bremen, run together with the Leibniz Centre for Tropical Marine Research (ZMT), which is enough to engage this literature seriously and to care that the model's framing is fair. The scientific grounding rests on the cited work; my contribution is turning that literature into a transparent, inspectable learning experience, rather than asserting authority over reef theory.

Appendix: for those who want to check the engine

For readers who would rather check the core than take "verified numerically" on trust, here is what sits under the hood, kept brief. Two coupled ordinary differential equations are integrated forward in time, with the cover fractions kept valid after each step:

dM/dt = a·M·C − g·M/(M + T) + γ·M·T dC/dt = r·T·C − d·C − a·M·C

with a the rate macroalgae overgrow coral, g the grazing rate of herbivorous fish, γ the spread of macroalgae over turf, r coral growth onto turf, and d coral mortality. Base parameters follow the source model (a = 0.10, γ = 0.80, r = 1.0, d = 0.24). Each slider reshapes these parameters, and the state then evolves on its own:

Fishing g = (1 − s) · 0.9 + 0.02 grazing Pollution a = 0.10 + s · 0.20 overgrowth γ = 0.80 + s · 0.40 spread Temperature d = 0.24 + s · 0.15 mortality plus a heat-stress mortality added to d while temperature stays above the bleaching threshold, re-arming in pulses

Macroalgae is floored at a small background value (about 0.02), standing for the constant arrival of algal propagules; without it a fully cleared reef could never be re-colonised and the tipping point would be unreachable from the coral side. Bistability and hysteresis are checked by sweeping the grazing rate g and integrating to equilibrium from both a coral-dominated and an algae-dominated start: coral collapses below about g ≈ 0.06, but recovery from the algal state requires grazing back up to about g ≈ 0.22. The gap between those two thresholds is the hysteresis. The sweep is reproducible from the browser console via verifyBistability().
