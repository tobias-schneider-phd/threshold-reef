Threshold Reef

A reef you can push past its tipping point

Tobias Schneider, PhD · Scientific Learning Experience Designer

A coral reef is one of the fastest changing ecosystems on Earth, and one of the hardest to explain without a wall of jargon. Threshold Reef is an attempt to let a curious person feel one of the most important and least intuitive ideas in reef resilience: that a reef can have more than one stable state, and that the way back is not the way in. You move three levers, watch a living reef respond in accelerated time, push it too far, and discover that easing off does not simply undo the damage.

This page is the part the simulation deliberately keeps off the screen. It is for reef scientists, instructional designers, and anyone who wants to check what is under the hood: what is modelled, what is simplified, why those simplifications serve the learning goal, and where the limits are. Being open about what is simplified is itself a piece of scientific integrity, and it is the part most science communication quietly skips.

What it is

A standalone, browser-based simulation of a coral reef under three pressures, built as a piece of science communication ("science for the living room"). Not a game with a score; an experience with a lesson.

Who it is for

In the simulation itself: a curious layperson, from interested adults to school and early-university students. Around it, on pages like this one: scientists and institutes who want to see that the science underneath is solid.

Format and tooling

A single standalone HTML file. Vanilla HTML, CSS and JavaScript, no build step, no frameworks, no external libraries. The reef is drawn procedurally as SVG and recoloured live, so bleaching is the coral itself losing its colour, not a swapped image.

Status

Built and playable. The model engine is complete and its bistability and hysteresis are verified numerically (see below).

💡

The idea

The brand claim of this piece is simple: it is a real dynamical simulation, not a reactive slideshow. That distinction is the whole point. A reactive piece reads the sliders and paints a matching picture, so every change is instantly reversible and nothing can ever truly be lost. That is a lookup table, not a simulation. Threshold Reef instead carries genuine state that integrates over time, and that state has a tipping point. Once the reef falls into the algae-dominated basin, easing a stressor back partway does not bring it back; recovery needs conditions pushed well beyond the point where it tipped, and even then the reef returns slowly rather than at once. That asymmetry is not a bug or a dramatic flourish; it is the lesson, and it emerges from the state-based dynamics rather than from a scripted visual response.

The emotional high point is therefore not the collapse. It is the moment after the collapse, when the player eases the pressure back to where the reef was thriving a minute ago and the reef stays down. The same settings that held a thriving reef no longer lift it out of the algae state; pulling it back takes more, and it takes time. That is when "tipping point" stops being a phrase and becomes something the person has felt.

🛠️

What I built

The learner has three levers, each a familiar pressure on real reefs: temperature (marine heat and bleaching), fishing (loss of the herbivorous fish that graze algae), and pollution (nutrient runoff that fertilises algae). They act on one reef in compressed time. The intro frames a run loosely as managing the reef "for one decade" to set the stakes, but there is no countdown and no endpoint; the run is open-ended, so a player can push the reef over and coax it back as often as they like, in any order.

The reef is alive on screen: coral colonies of several growth forms sway, fish school and thin out as grazers are lost, the water turns murky as nutrients rise, and coral pales and bleaches as the water warms. The reef also speaks. A status badge moves through Healthy, Stressed, Bleaching and Critical, and short narration lines name what is happening and why, for instance that protected grazers are keeping algae in check while the coral bleaches, or that bringing the reef back from the algae state takes strong grazing and time rather than a quick change. So this is not a silent simulation the learner must decode unaided; the system tells its own story in plain language as it unfolds.

The intended emotional arc, though nothing forces it, runs like this: the learner moves the sliders and the reef responds; they push a pressure too far; the reef tips into algal dominance; they try to undo it by easing the pressure back; it does not bounce back easily; and an optional "The Science" panel explains why. In practice people explore freely and may tip and recover the reef many times before they ever look further. The panel is always one click away rather than unlocked by a particular state, so explanation is there whenever the person reaches for it, ideally after they have felt the tipping point rather than before.

🎨

The design approach

Experience before explanation. The simulation teaches by consequence, not by instruction. There is no "correct" or "incorrect"; there is a reef that thrives or collapses, and a quiet narration that names what is happening and why.
Built around agency. The piece is deliberately framed so the person feels able to act rather than merely informed of a disaster. Local protection visibly matters: in this implementation grazing is the dominant recovery lever, reflecting the source model's focus on herbivory and algal feedbacks; the reef can recover within the simulation, and Reset is always available. The aim is for the crisis to read as serious and clear without being dramatised.
Rigour that is honest about its limits. Where the model simplifies, this page says so, in plain terms, below.

The science behind it

I include this depth so the engine can be inspected and checked, not to claim the modelling as my own.

The engine is based on the analytical core of the coral–macroalgae–turf model of Mumby, Hastings & Edwards (2007), a foundational result on the resilience of Caribbean reefs. That paper actually contains two models: a spatially explicit, demographically detailed simulation, and a reduced analytical model of three competing space-occupiers. This simulation uses the reduced analytical model. Three groups compete for space on the reef floor, and their cover fractions sum to one:

Ccoral cover
Mmacroalgal cover
Tshort, cropped algal turf: the grazed space that coral and macroalgae compete over, taken as the remainder, T = 1 − C − M. In the original this is turf kept short by grazers, not bare rock, so calling it simply "open substrate" would be a slight misstatement.

Two coupled ordinary differential equations are integrated forward in time with a fixed Euler time step on each animation frame, a fixed model-time step per frame rather than one scaled to wall-clock time:

dM/dt = a·M·C − g·M/(M + T) + γ·M·T dC/dt = r·T·C − d·C − a·M·C

with a the rate at which macroalgae overgrow coral, g the grazing rate of herbivorous fish, γ the spread of macroalgae over turf (written c in the original), r coral recruitment and growth onto turf, and d coral mortality. The base parameters follow the source model (a = 0.10, γ = 0.80, r = 1.0, d = 0.24).

Two honest notes for a reader who knows this model. First, temperature and nutrients are not state or control variables in the original analytical model. The three levers here are deliberate didactic extensions of it: fishing changes grazing g; nutrient pollution raises macroalgal growth and spread (a and γ); temperature raises coral mortality d and adds a heat-stress term above a bleaching threshold. Second, Mumby et al. treat bleaching as an episode of acute coral mortality and disturbance, not as a separate temperature-driven term in the equations; the explicit heat term here is again a teaching device, not a literal feature of the source model.

The three levers do not set the reef's state directly; they reshape the parameters, and the state then evolves on its own:

🌡️

Temperature raises baseline coral mortality d, and above a bleaching threshold it adds a heat-stress mortality term that re-arms while the water stays hot, so sustained heat keeps damaging coral rather than acting once. Visually, the coral is recoloured toward white as it bleaches.

🎣

Fishing lowers grazing: g = (1 − fishing) · 0.9 + 0.02. More fishing means fewer herbivores, so less algae is eaten.

🏭

Pollution raises macroalgal growth and spread: a and γ both increase with nutrient load. Runoff acts as fertiliser for algae.

Bistability and hysteresis are the whole point, and in this model they are verified numerically. Sweeping the grazing rate g from both a coral-dominated and an algae-dominated starting condition reveals two different tipping points: coral collapses below a grazing rate of about g ≈ 0.06, but recovery from the algal state requires grazing back up to about g ≈ 0.22. The gap between those thresholds is the hysteresis: the reef does not retrace its path, and recovery demands conditions pushed well past the point at which it fell over. In play, this is why turning temperature or pollution back down is not enough once the reef has tipped; grazing is the lever that governs whether recovery is even possible.

A word of scientific honesty

The theory and mechanism of bistability and hysteresis on reefs are well grounded, but the empirical evidence for true hysteresis on real reefs is mixed, scale-dependent and not universal (Bruno et al. 2009; Dudgeon et al. 2010; Mumby, Steneck & Hastings 2013; Schmitt et al. 2019). Some Caribbean settings with weakened herbivory and strong algal feedbacks fit the picture well; elsewhere, many coral-to-algae transitions are better read as reversible phase shifts maintained by ongoing stress than as two states that are both stable under the same conditions. This simulation therefore presents hysteresis as a well-founded mechanism and teaching frame, not as a settled law that holds identically on every reef.

Numerical implementation and verification

For readers who would rather check the engine than take "verified numerically" on trust, here is what sits under the hood. Each slider position (normalised to a value s between 0 and 1) maps to the model parameters like this:

Fishing g = (1 − s) · 0.9 + 0.02 grazing, about 0.02 to 0.92 Pollution a = 0.10 + s · 0.20 overgrowth, 0.10 to 0.30 γ = 0.80 + s · 0.40 spread, 0.80 to 1.20 Temperature d = 0.24 + s · 0.15 mortality, 0.24 to 0.39 plus a heat-stress mortality of 0.8 added to d while the temperature stays above the bleaching threshold (s > 0.60), re-arming in short pulses so sustained heat keeps acting

The live simulation advances the model by a fixed time step on each animation frame, which keeps each frame deterministic. The wall-clock pace can vary slightly with the display's frame rate, but the phase-space behaviour and the verified hysteresis thresholds are the same regardless, and they, not a precise real-time rate, are the intended learning object. After every step the cover fractions are kept valid: C and M are held between 0 and 1, their sum is capped at 1, and M is floored at a small background value (about 0.02) standing for the constant arrival of algal propagules. That floor is a deliberate addition to the source equations: without it M = 0 would be an absorbing state, a fully cleared reef could never be re-colonised, and the tipping point would be unreachable from the coral side.

The two thresholds are found by sweeping the grazing rate g from 0.90 down to 0 in steps of 0.02. At each value the reduced model is integrated to equilibrium from two starting conditions, one coral-dominated (C = 0.70, M = 0.05) and one algae-dominated (C = 0.05, M = 0.70), with the base parameters held fixed. An endpoint counts as coral-dominated when coral cover settles above 0.4. The coral start first collapses to algae at about g ≈ 0.06, the collapse threshold; the algae start first recovers to coral only at about g ≈ 0.22, the recovery threshold. Because recovery needs more grazing than collapse, both states are stable across the gap between those values, and that gap is the hysteresis. The sweep is reproducible from the browser console via verifyBistability().

What is simplified, and why on purpose

This is a qualitative teaching model, not a research or forecasting tool. The simplifications are deliberate, and each one buys clarity:

One reef, not many reef types. The original model was parameterised for structurally complex, mid-depth Caribbean forereefs; this piece does not represent other habitats.
Abstract, accelerated time, not calendar time. There is no fixed time unit, no countdown and no endpoint; the "decade" in the intro is only a framing device. The full Mumby simulation, by contrast, steps in six-month intervals and includes disturbances such as hurricanes, which this core deliberately omits.
Fishing is represented as loss of herbivory. Real fishing is broader, but the decisive effect here is the removal of grazers, the lever that controls the tipping point.
Pollution is represented as nutrient enrichment, as one clean lever. In reality nutrient effects are strongly context-dependent and usually entangled with herbivory rather than acting as one uniform driver (Rasher et al. 2012).
Bleaching is heat-driven coral stress and mortality, not a full physiological model. Real bleaching depends on cumulative heat, light, taxon, symbiont type, prior damage and follow-on processes such as disease.
Turf T stands for cropped algal turf, not the whole universe of non-coral states. Real reefs can also shift into states dominated by sponges, soft corals, corallimorpharians or urchin barrens (Norström et al. 2009).
Not represented at all: spatial structure and neighbourhood effects, larval dispersal, sedimentation, ocean acidification, disease, coral species and functional-group differences, and hydrodynamics.

The reason for holding the line is the same as the reason for the piece. The goal is not ecological completeness; it is to make one well-established idea, alternative stable states and the asymmetry of recovery, visible and legible to a non-specialist. Every extra variable would make the model more realistic and less teachable, and past a point it would bury the one idea the piece exists to convey.

Limits of interpretation

Threshold Reef should be read as a conceptual instrument, not a prediction. It is built on the Caribbean-derived analytical core of a single model and is not calibrated to any particular reef, so the thresholds it shows are heuristic learning objects, not universal, site-independent numbers. Region and habitat matter: thresholds and resilience differ between regions, and Caribbean values should not be sold as global defaults (Mumby et al. 2016). The cleanest one-line statement of what the piece is: it is modelled on a classic Caribbean coral–macroalgal mechanism that can produce bistability and hysteresis under certain conditions, and it serves understanding of feedbacks and tipping points, not prediction of specific reef trajectories.

A note on how this was built

My work here is as a Scientific Learning Experience Designer with a biology PhD, not as a reef modeller. The background in tropical marine ecology comes from graduate study in the ISATEC programme at the University of Bremen, run together with the Leibniz Centre for Tropical Marine Research (ZMT), which is enough to engage this literature seriously and to care that the model's framing is fair. The scientific grounding rests on the cited work; my contribution is turning that literature into a transparent, inspectable learning experience, rather than asserting authority over reef theory.

Why reefs, why now

Coral reefs occupy a thin sliver of the ocean, yet a large share of marine life and the livelihoods of hundreds of millions of people depend on them. The pressure on them is not a single recent emergency but a long, worsening trend: mass-bleaching events have recurred and intensified over the past three decades, from the late 1990s and the severe years of 2016 to 2017 through to the 2023 to 2025 global event, the most extensive yet recorded, which exposed roughly 84 percent of the world's reef area to bleaching-level heat across all three ocean basins before NOAA judged it to have likely ended in mid-2025 (ICRI 2025; NOAA Coral Reef Watch 2026). The IPCC's Special Report on Global Warming of 1.5°C projects that warm-water reefs would decline by 70 to 90 percent at 1.5°C of warming, and that more than 99 percent would be lost at 2°C (IPCC 2018). The 2023 to 2025 event is best understood as the most recent and largest reminder of that trajectory, not as a sudden new crisis. A piece that lets a person feel how a reef tips, and how much harder it is to bring back than to lose, is a small contribution to why that matters.
